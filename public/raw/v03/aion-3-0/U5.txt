The walls began their slow respiration around the forty-minute mark. At first, I tried to rationalize it—some trick of light, my own pulse reflected in my vision—but then the plaster itself swelled and contracted, and I knew this was something else entirely.

Colors bled from the framed print above my couch, Matisse's blue nymphs swimming across the white wall, leaving trails of cobalt that dripped toward the ceiling. I laughed then, a laugh that felt like it originated from somewhere below my stomach, from some pre-human part of me that remembered how to be amazed.

Then the edges began to blur. Not just visually—the boundary between my skin and the air dissolved. I couldn't locate the precise point where "I" ended and the room began. My thoughts became audible, then visible, then indistinguishable from the music playing somewhere. I was the music. I was the breathing walls. I was the trail of blue nymphs.

And that's when the terror came—not fear exactly, but the vertigo of existing without a container, without a self to anchor experience. I was everything and therefore nothing. I dissolved.

Then I remembered how to breathe, and the walls breathed with me.
